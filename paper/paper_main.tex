\begin{abstract}
Public radar target recognition datasets are commonly evaluated with random train/test partitions over individual range--Doppler frames. Because frames come from continuous recordings, this never asks whether a classifier generalises to an acquisition it has not seen. We report an acquisition-level audit of the Real Doppler RAD-DAR database, reconstructing its acquisition structure into 52 grouping units from 80 folders. Under a session-grouped protocol, aggregate accuracy is close to that under random partitioning and we do not establish a significant difference. The per-acquisition breakdown is nevertheless strongly heterogeneous: of twenty-one drone acquisitions, twenty attain out-of-fold recall between 0.90 and 0.99 while one attains 0.249. In a controlled experiment excluding that acquisition from training entirely, recall on a held-back half is 0.178 over ten seeds; adding the other half to training raises it to 0.948. The same experiment on the weakest acquisition of each other class recovers 0.056 and 0.008, so a large recovery is not a generic property of a difficult recording. The acquisition is distinguished from all others by the Doppler position of its target energy. A follow-up compression study finds that fourfold Doppler-bin aggregation raises recall on this held-out acquisition from 0.222 to 0.398 with no detected change in standard-split recall. Three further results are negative and reported in full: a mechanism tested for that effect is refuted, the effect does not generalise to four other held-out acquisitions, and no interaction is detected between the two quantisation axes. We present this as a single-acquisition case study and argue that acquisition-level breakdowns should accompany aggregate accuracy on benchmarks assembled from continuous recordings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Automatic target recognition from frequency modulated continuous wave (FMCW) radar has been widely addressed with convolutional networks operating on range--Doppler representations. On public benchmarks, these systems routinely report accuracies close to 99\,\%, and the reported figure is generally obtained by partitioning the available frames at random.
That partitioning choice deserves scrutiny. Radar datasets are assembled from continuous recordings, so consecutive frames within one acquisition are strongly correlated. A random partition places frames from a single acquisition on both sides of the split, and the reported accuracy therefore describes performance on acquisitions the model has already seen. It does not describe performance on a new deployment, a new day, or a new target trajectory, which is the quantity a system integrator actually needs.
This paper re-evaluates a widely used public dataset under a protocol that holds out whole acquisitions. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A reproducible preparation procedure for the Real Doppler RAD-DAR database that removes an exact duplication present in the distributed archive and recovers the acquisition structure from the directory hierarchy (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
\item A five-fold comparison of random against session-grouped partitioning, in which we do not establish a significant aggregate difference, together with a discussion of why such comparisons are difficult to test properly (Section~\ref{sec:leak}).
\item Identification of a single drone acquisition on which the classifier fails almost completely under acquisition-level evaluation, together with a controlled exposure experiment indicating that the failure is associated with the absence of comparable training examples (Section~\ref{sec:regime}).
\item A follow-up compression study. Fourfold Doppler aggregation improves transfer to the held-out acquisition with no detected change in standard-split accuracy, an effect we establish statistically for that acquisition but cannot explain and cannot reproduce on four others; a mechanism test and an interaction test are both negative (Section~\ref{sec:compress}).
\end{enumerate}
We emphasise the negative results deliberately. Several physically motivated hypotheses were formulated during this work and each was refuted by measurement; reporting only the surviving result would misrepresent what the data supports.
We also state the scope plainly. Both principal observations concern the same acquisition, so the effective sample size at the level of acquisitions is one. This is a case study of one benchmark, not a general result about radar target recognition.

\section{Related Work}
The Real Doppler RAD-DAR database was released with a convolutional baseline, DopplerNet, reporting 99.48\,\% accuracy on the three-class problem of vehicles, drones and pedestrians~\cite{roldan2020}. The acquisition hardware is described separately~\cite{quevedo2019}. A recent lightweight architecture reports 98.08\,\% average accuracy on the same data with a model footprint of roughly 400\,kB~\cite{zhang2025}, and provides the per-class confusion matrices we use as an external reference in Section~\ref{sec:baseline}. 
That correlated samples inflate apparent performance is not a new observation. Grouped partitioning is standard practice in human activity recognition, where subject-disjoint splits are routine, and radar work has previously excluded segments of the same track from different folds~\cite{buchman2022}. Our methodological contribution is therefore not the idea of grouping but its application to this widely reused benchmark, and the specific failure it exposes.

Comparing learning procedures across resampled partitions is also known to be statistically delicate. Dietterich showed that several commonly used tests have inflated Type~I error on cross-validation scores~\cite{dietterich1998}, and Nadeau and Bengio showed that the dependence introduced by overlapping training sets must be accounted for in any variance estimate~\cite{nadeau2003}. We take both cautions seriously in Section~\ref{sec:leak} and deliberately do not rest any claim on a resampling-based significance test.

Separately, the observation that model compression damages some classes far more than others is established in the vision domain~\cite{hooker2019,hooker2020,tran2022}. Our compression study was originally motivated as a radar instantiation of that phenomenon; we report in Section~\ref{sec:compress} that we did not reproduce it.

\section{Dataset and Preparation}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Acquisition}

The database was collected with an FMCW system at 8.75\,GHz with 500\,MHz of bandwidth, using one transmitting and eight receiving antennas forming a 64-element array~\cite{roldan2020,quevedo2019}. The published parameters give a range resolution of 0.878\,m, a maximum unambiguous range of 3.596\,km and a Doppler resolution of 0.34\,km/h with 512 integrated ramps. We quote these rather than recomputing them: a naive calculation from bandwidth alone gives a range resolution roughly three times finer than the system achieves.

Samples are supplied as $11 \times 61$ matrices obtained by applying a constant false alarm rate detector to the full range--Doppler matrix and cropping around each detection. The retained Doppler window spans approximately 21\,km/h, considerably narrower than the extent associated with rotor blade returns from a small rotorcraft, so the crops do not retain what is needed to analyse blade-flash structure. We note this because it constrains what mechanism arguments the data can support, not as a claim that the crops contain no micro-Doppler information at all.

\subsection{Duplication in the distributed archive}

The distributed archive contains the complete directory tree twice, once beneath a \texttt{data/} prefix and once at the archive root. Content hashing of each acquisition folder identifies and removes the duplication, after which the totals are 5720 vehicle, 5065 drone and 6700 pedestrian samples, or 17\,485 in total, matching the published figure exactly~\cite{zhang2025}. A second pass removes any folder whose name duplicates another of the same class but which holds fewer files, which guards against a truncated copy left by an interrupted extraction.

\subsection{Recovering acquisition structure}

The acquisition structure is not encoded in file names, which are simple running indices. It is encoded in the directory hierarchy: folders are named by acquisition time, with suffixes distinguishing variants recorded within the same session, for example \texttt{13-13} and \texttt{13-13p}, or \texttt{15-55}, \texttt{15-55a}, \texttt{15-55m} and \texttt{15-55p}.

We group by the leading \texttt{HH-MM} timestamp. This is a fact about when the data was collected and cannot drift with a parameter choice. An earlier attempt to group by content similarity failed badly and is reported here as a caution: because every sample is cropped around its own detection, the mean feature vector of any acquisition resembles a central blob, and all acquisitions correlate above 0.9 regardless of provenance. Grouping by timestamp yields 15 vehicle, 21 drone and 16 pedestrian units, 52 in total, from 80 folders.

The rule is an inference from directory structure, not a ground-truth acquisition identifier, and we treat it as an assumption. Two observations support it. The suffix pattern is systematic, with pedestrian folders appearing as matched \texttt{f} and \texttt{i} pairs, and several suffixed pairs share a file count \emph{and} peak-to-floor statistics identical to one decimal place, for instance \texttt{17-09} and \texttt{17-09p} at 140 files and 41.4\,dB; every such pair falls within one unit under our rule. The rule is also conservative, since merging independent recordings can only make the evaluation harder. A reader preferring folder-level grouping can reproduce either choice from the released partition files.

\section{Model and Protocol}
\label{sec:baseline}

\subsection{Architecture and normalisation}

We use a compact depthwise-separable convolutional network of 101\,139 parameters, sized to match the 101\,419-parameter reference model reported on this dataset~\cite{zhang2025} so that its behaviour is representative of a network that would plausibly be deployed.

Input normalisation was selected by ablation over grouped folds (Table~\ref{tab:norm}). A fixed affine offset outperformed peak-referenced normalisation by 1.7 accuracy points, and was additionally preferred because, being a fixed affine map, quantising before or after normalisation is equivalent up to a scale factor, which keeps the compression analysis of Section~\ref{sec:compress} interpretable.

\subsection{Partitioning}

Under the \emph{grouped} protocol, whole timestamp units are assigned to train, validation, or test; no unit contributes samples to more than one subset. Folds are balanced by sample count rather than unit count, since unit sizes range from 31 to 660 files. Under the \emph{random} protocol, individual frames are shuffled, with fold sizes matched per class to the
grouped folds.

Validation data is drawn from whole training units, never from training frames: validating on random frames under a grouped protocol would reintroduce the correlation the protocol exists to remove, and every early-stopping decision would then be taken on contaminated data. Splits are verified by content hash rather than by path, since duplicate folders make path-level verification unsound.

\section{Is Random Partitioning Optimistic?}
\label{sec:leak}

Table~\ref{tab:kfold} reports five folds by three seeds under each protocol. The design separates two sources of variation: \emph{fold} standard deviation measures sensitivity to which units are held out, and \emph{seed} standard deviation measures sensitivity to initialisation alone.

Under grouped partitioning, the mean accuracy is 0.9099 against 0.9335, and mean drone recall is 0.8376 against 0.9358. These differences are not trivial, but they are small relative to the fold-to-fold spread: the grouped standard deviation is 0.0427 for accuracy and 0.1389 for drone recall, roughly eight and eighteen times the random-partition figures.

We rest no claim on a significance test here. Fold scores under the two protocols are not naturally paired, since a grouped fold is defined by which acquisitions it contains while a random fold is defined by a shuffle, and cross-validation scores are dependent through overlapping training sets~\cite{dietterich1998,nadeau2003}. A test treating the folds as paired independent observations would overstate its confidence. For the record it gives $t=-1.19$ for accuracy and $t=-1.12$ for drone F1, neither significant
even were the test sound.

\textbf{We therefore report that we did not establish a significant aggregate difference between the protocols, which is a weaker statement than the absence of one.} What the data does show clearly is heterogeneity. The per-fold differences in drone F1 are $+0.003$, $-0.023$, $+0.007$, $-0.226$ and $-0.010$: the aggregate difference between protocols is dominated by a single fold rather than appearing as a consistent shift. Section~\ref{sec:regime} identifies what that fold contains.

Every other drone acquisition places its target energy at Doppler centroid between 29.7 and 31.2, at the centre of the 61-bin axis, and holds it there: the within-acquisition standard deviation ranges from 0.64 to 2.37 bins. Acquisition \texttt{13-48} places it at 8.50, and with a standard deviation of 10.64 bins, four and a half times the largest spread any other acquisition shows. It is separated from the other twenty on both where its target energy sits and how much that position varies. A nearest-centroid check finds 100\,\% of its samples closest to the drone class centroid, so a labelling error is not indicated.

The source publication describes three drone exercises: radial trajectories flown on autopilot to 3.1\,km, a circular trajectory at 800\,m range, and a free flight with speed and altitude varied manually~\cite{roldan2020}; the hardware paper likewise reports a free flight among its tests~\cite{quevedo2019}. Highly variable radial velocity is what manual free flight produces, and \texttt{13-48} is the only acquisition whose Doppler scatter is an order of magnitude above the rest. We therefore consider it likely to be that exercise, while noting that the publication gives no timestamps and that a per-recording processing difference cannot be excluded from the published data alone.

\subsection{Coverage test}

To separate absence of comparable training data from intrinsic difficulty, we split \texttt{13-48} in half. In the first condition, the acquisition is excluded from training entirely and the model is evaluated on one half; in the second, the other half is added to the training set.

Recall rises from 0.178 to 0.948 over ten seeds, a recovery of 0.770 (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}). Under the held-out condition 79\,\% of the samples are assigned to the pedestrian class; with half the acquisition in training, 3\,\% are. The effect is far larger than its seed-to-seed spread: held-out recall ranges from 0.142 to 0.264 across the ten seeds and the half-in-training condition from 0.904 to 0.980, so the two conditions do not overlap on any seed.

The experiment indicates that the failure is associated with the absence of comparable training examples rather than with an irreducible inability to classify samples of this kind. We stop short of a stronger causal statement. Because the added training samples come from the same acquisition, the experiment cannot separate an out-of-distribution flight regime from an acquisition-specific signature that the network learns, nor from a per-recording processing difference; it establishes that something present in this acquisition and absent from the other twenty is learnable and sufficient.

\subsection{A control acquisition}

A large recovery is only evidence of a coverage failure if a large recovery is unusual. Acquisitions well below the aggregate exist in the other two classes: the weakest pedestrian and vehicle acquisitions sit at 0.7776 and 0.8189. We therefore repeated the identical experiment, at the same ten seeds, on the weakest acquisition in either non-drone class, \texttt{11-23}.

Held out entirely it reaches 0.800, and with half of it in training 0.856, a recovery of 0.056 against the 0.770 of \texttt{13-48}. We repeated this once more on \texttt{15-37}, the weakest vehicle acquisition, which recovers 0.008: its held-out and half-in-training conditions overlap on nine of ten seeds and the per-seed difference has a standard deviation of 0.030, so its recovery is not distinguishable from zero. All three acquisitions sit well below their class aggregate, and exposure repairs one while leaving the other two where they were. Low recall and a large recovery are therefore separate properties, and the coverage result is not a restatement of \texttt{13-48} being difficult.

The two controls also differ from \texttt{13-48} descriptively. \texttt{11-23} is unremarkable in Doppler and an outlier in extent, with nearly three times the number of cells within 20\,dB of its peak ($z=+3.4$), and its samples sit nearest the \emph{drone} centroid for 56.8\,\% of frames; we read it as confusable with another class rather than unrepresented, a failure adding training data should not repair. \texttt{15-37} is the cleaner control: no property we measured separates it from the other vehicle acquisitions at all, the largest standardised difference across the twelve being $0.25$ against $-15.67$ for the Doppler centroid of \texttt{13-48}, and 62.7\,\% of its frames sit nearest their own class. It is descriptively ordinary and simply handled less well, and exposure does not change that. An acquisition can therefore be weak without being unrepresented, which is the alternative the coverage experiment has to exclude. Three acquisitions of fifty-two remain three cases, and we do not claim these failure modes exhaust the possibilities.

This is the paper's principal result. A classifier benchmarked on this dataset with random partitioning reports accuracy near 99\,\% while failing almost completely on one acquisition, and the standard evaluation gives no indication of it. If that acquisition corresponds to a distinct flight regime, as the Doppler evidence and the source publication's description together suggest, the practical consequence for a counter-UAS deployment is direct. We note that this consequence follows from an identification we cannot verify, and treat it accordingly.

\section{A Follow-Up Compression Study}
\label{sec:compress}

The experiments in this section were motivated by the gap identified in Section~\ref{sec:regime} and should be read as a targeted follow-up to it rather than as an independent study.

\subsection{Design}

We examined two compression axes. \emph{Input resolution} aggregates adjacent Doppler bins by factors of 1, 2, 4 and 8. Aggregation sums linear power and returns to decibels; it is an incoherent operation and is \emph{not} equivalent to shortening the coherent processing interval, since the dataset supplies magnitude only and the phase required for that operation is not present. We report it strictly as a reduction of input tensor size, which is a genuine memory cost on an embedded target. \emph{Numerical precision} applies uniform quantisation to input and weights at 32, 16, 8 and 4 bits.

Each grid cell is evaluated under two settings: the standard grouped split, and a setting in which \texttt{13-48} is excluded from training and used as the sole test set.

The full $4\times4$ grid was run at two seeds; Table~\ref{tab:agg} reports a ten-seed follow-up restricted to 32 and 8 bits. Four-bit weight quantisation proved unstable, with an accuracy standard deviation of 0.1757 across seeds, so its cells reflect training failures rather than a systematic effect and we do not interpret them; 16-bit results lay within one standard deviation of 32-bit throughout. The complete two-seed grid is in the released artefact.

Two features of Table~\ref{tab:agg} deserve comment. First, standard-split drone recall lies between 0.901 and 0.932 across every aggregation level and both bit widths. Comparing fourfold against full resolution on the standard split gives a Welch $t$ of $0.79$ at 32 bits and $0.16$ at 8 bits over ten seeds, so we do not detect a change; we note that this is a failure to detect a difference and not a demonstration of equivalence, which would require a non-inferiority margin we have not specified. Second, recall on the unseen acquisition is not flat at all: it rises from 0.222 at full resolution to 0.398 at fourfold aggregation and then collapses to 0.192 at eightfold, at 32 bits, with the same pattern at 8 bits.

A Welch test on fourfold against full resolution gives $t=+4.17$ at 32 bits and $t=+3.08$ at 8 bits over ten seeds. A rank-based check and a bootstrap confidence interval on the difference of means, computed on the first five seeds, gave $[+0.049,+0.307]$ and $[+0.058,+0.392]$ respectively, both excluding zero. Per-seed values are consistent with a single wide distribution rather than a split one, so the effect is a shift in the mean accompanied by strong seed dependence, not a subset of runs succeeding while others fail.

\textbf{We therefore report that fourfold Doppler aggregation improves transfer to this held-out acquisition, with no detected change in standard-split recall and a fourfold reduction in input tensor size.} The qualifier is important: Section~\ref{sec:compress}C shows that the effect does not generalise to other held-out acquisitions. The improvement is substantial in relative terms, close to a doubling, but leaves recall far below the 0.948 obtainable when the acquisition is represented in training (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}). It is a partial mitigation, not a solution.

\subsection{The effect does not generalise across acquisitions}

Aggregation improved three of the five, with a mean change of $+0.033$, a paired $t$ of $+1.16$ across acquisitions and an exact sign test giving $p=0.50$. \textbf{The effect does not generalise.}

One structural feature should be noted. The four other acquisitions begin between 0.800 and 0.934, leaving little room for improvement, whereas \texttt{13-48} begins at 0.217. The compatible reading is that aggregation recovered part of the gap in the one case where the classifier was failing and made no consistent difference where it was already performing. With a single failing acquisition we cannot distinguish an effect specific to failing acquisitions from one specific to this acquisition, and we do not claim the former.

\subsection{Two further negative results}

\emph{We cannot distinguish an interaction between the quantisation axes from zero.} At four bits and fourfold aggregation, quantising the input alone costs 0.0159 in accuracy and quantising the weights alone costs 0.1028; the measured joint cost is 0.1412 against an additive prediction of 0.1187. The interaction contrast $I = Y_{\mathrm{both}} - Y_{\mathrm{input}} - Y_{\mathrm{weights}} + Y_{\mathrm{neither}}$ is $-0.0225$, meaning the joint cost exceeds the additive prediction by that amount. A bootstrap confidence interval over the per-seed runs gives $[-0.185, +0.161]$, comfortably including zero, with an approximate $t$ of $-0.23$.

We therefore report that no interaction is detected, which is not the same as establishing additivity. The interval is wide because four-bit weight quantisation is unstable: accuracy standard deviations are 0.176 in the weights-only condition and 0.134 in the joint condition, driven by individual seeds collapsing to 0.534 and 0.585. The test has little power, and a moderate interaction would have escaped it.

\emph{Doppler-shift augmentation does not reproduce the effect, so the obvious explanation fails.} Acquisition \texttt{13-48} is distinguished from the rest chiefly by the Doppler position of its target energy (Table~\ref{tab:profile}), which suggests that aggregation helps by making the classifier less sensitive to that position. If so, augmenting training with Doppler shifts should reproduce the effect at full resolution. Shifts fill vacated cells with the sample's own noise floor rather than wrapping, since a circular shift is not a physical operation. At shifts of $\pm4$, $\pm8$ and $\pm16$ bins, unseen-regime recall was 0.1608, 0.1613 and 0.1603 respectively, against a baseline of 0.2214: consistently slightly worse, while standard-split recall was unaffected. The hypothesis is refuted. We therefore report the aggregation effect as an empirical finding and offer no mechanism for it.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\subsection{What the results support}

The coverage result is the finding we consider established: a measured effect of 0.77 in recall, a controlled manipulation isolating the cause, and an independent descriptive signature identifying the acquisition without reference to classifier performance.

The recommendation follows, with its scope stated. For datasets assembled from continuous acquisitions, where deployment requires generalisation to recordings or conditions absent from training, aggregate accuracy should be accompanied by an acquisition-level breakdown; it is a by-product of a single cross-validation pass. Where deployment genuinely operates on the benchmark's own distribution, frame-level evaluation may be appropriate, and we make no claim against it.

The aggregation result is established for one acquisition, is not understood, and does not reproduce on four others. We report it because a reader repeating this audit will want to know the comparison was run and how it came out, and because the asymmetry in Table~\ref{tab:loo} is worth testing where more than one failing acquisition is available. We do not present it as a recommendation for practice.

\subsection{What they do not support}

Four physically motivated mechanisms were formulated during this work to explain why the drone class should be more fragile, and all four were refuted by measurement: rotor sideband loss, refuted by the Doppler span of the supplied crops; low redundancy from compact energy, refuted once concentration was measured relative to the peak rather than to total power; limited dynamic-range headroom, refuted because the apparent correlation between per-acquisition recall and headroom (Pearson $-0.62$) falls to $-0.16$ once \texttt{13-48} is excluded, and the rank correlation was never significant; and Doppler positional insensitivity, refuted by the augmentation experiment above. We report these because the pattern is informative: plausible physical stories about this dataset are easy to construct and difficult to sustain.

\subsection{Limitations}

Both findings rest on the same single acquisition. The coverage result concerns \texttt{13-48} directly, and the aggregation result is measured by transfer to \texttt{13-48}, so the paper has an effective sample size of one at the level of flight regimes. Whether fourfold aggregation would improve transfer to a \emph{different} unrepresented regime is untested, and we cannot claim it. The data comes from one dataset, one radar and one drone platform, and the acquisition comprises 7.8\,\% of the drone samples. Whether the distinguishing Doppler offset reflects flight dynamics or a per-recording processing difference cannot be determined from the published data. The identification of \texttt{13-48} with the free-flight exercise is an inference, since the source publication does not supply acquisition timestamps. The two controls in Section~\ref{sec:regime}D show the recovery is not generic, but three acquisitions of fifty-two have been tested this way, and the contrast between them is a contrast of three cases. Our grouping rule assumes that a shared timestamp prefix implies a shared acquisition. Section~\ref{sec:data}C sets out the evidence for it, but it remains an inference from directory structure rather than a ground-truth acquisition identifier.

We tested directly whether the aggregation result generalises beyond \texttt{13-48} and found that it does not (Table~\ref{tab:loo}). Aggregation improved three of five held-out acquisitions, with a mean change of $+0.033$ and an exact sign test giving $p=0.50$. The aggregation claim is therefore stated as specific to \texttt{13-48} throughout this paper. Whether the effect would appear for a different acquisition on which the classifier fails is untested, and cannot be tested within this dataset, which contains only one such acquisition.

Our classifier reaches 0.9335 under random partitioning, below the 99.48\,\% reported for DopplerNet and the 98.08\,\% for the lighter reference. We measured that gap rather than conceding it. Roughly two points are protocol, not model: 0.9335 is a five-fold cross-validated mean, and the same architecture on a single random partition, the quantity a single reported figure represents, reaches 0.9537. Capacity and schedule explain none of the rest. Across width multipliers of one, two and three, to 871\,731 parameters, and 40 and 80 epochs, the best configuration reaches 0.9546, which is 0.0009 above the architecture we use; accuracy plateaus and the model is not capacity limited here. That leaves about 2.6 points to the lighter baseline unexplained, most plausibly in preprocessing or in protocol details we could not reconstruct from the published descriptions. Our result is therefore demonstrated for a classifier within about 2.6 points of the published figures rather than for those models, and whether one at 98\,\% also fails on \texttt{13-48} remains the first thing we would test given a baseline implementation.

\section{Conclusion}

Re-evaluating a public radar target recognition benchmark at the level of acquisitions leaves aggregate accuracy close to that obtained under random partitioning, and we do not establish a significant aggregate difference. The per-acquisition breakdown is nevertheless strongly heterogeneous: one drone acquisition of twenty-one attains 0.249 out-of-fold recall against 0.90 to 0.99 for the remainder, and in a controlled experiment excluding it from training entirely, recall on a held-back half is 0.178, rising to 0.948 when the other half is added to training. The same experiment on the weakest acquisition of each other class recovers 0.056 and 0.008. The acquisition is distinguished by the Doppler position of its target energy. We suggest, without being able to verify it, that it corresponds to the manually flown free-flight exercise described in the source publication.

A follow-up compression study found that fourfold Doppler-bin aggregation raises recall on this held-out acquisition from 0.222 to 0.398 with no detected change in standard-split recall. Three further results are negative. The explanation we tested, reduced sensitivity to Doppler position, was refuted by an augmentation experiment. The effect itself improved only three of five acquisitions in a leave-one-acquisition-out repetition, with an exact sign test giving $p=0.50$, so it does not generalise. And no interaction is detected between the input and weight quantisation axes, in a test whose confidence interval is wide enough that a moderate interaction would have escaped it.

The practical conclusion is narrow and, we believe, sound: on benchmarks assembled from continuous acquisitions, a headline accuracy is compatible with near-complete failure on an individual acquisition, and a per-acquisition breakdown is inexpensive insurance against reporting one without knowing it. We present this as a case study of one dataset and one acquisition, and it should be read at that scope.

\section*{Reproducibility}

All preparation, partitioning, training and analysis code, the generated partition files and the per-stage reports are released at \url{https://github.com/falcon-colab/rdrd-acquisition-coverage}, archived at~\cite{dataset2026}.
