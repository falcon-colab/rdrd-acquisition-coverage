\begin{abstract}
Public radar target recognition datasets are commonly evaluated with random train/test partitions over individual range--Doppler frames. Because frames come from continuous recordings, this never asks whether a classifier generalises to an acquisition it has not seen. We report an acquisition-level audit of the Real Doppler RAD-DAR database, reconstructing its structure into 52 grouping units from 80 folders. Under a session-grouped protocol aggregate accuracy stays close to that under random partitioning, yet the per-acquisition breakdown is strongly heterogeneous: of twenty-one drone acquisitions, twenty attain out-of-fold recall between 0.90 and 0.99 while one attains 0.249. Excluding that acquisition from training entirely leaves recall on a held-back half at 0.178 over ten seeds; adding the other half raises it to 0.948, with no overlap between the conditions on any seed. The same experiment on the weakest acquisition of each other class recovers only 0.056 and 0.008. The acquisition is distinguished from all others by the Doppler position of its target energy. Rebuilding the published baseline's three-frame temporal input raises aggregate accuracy to 0.979 and still leaves that acquisition at 0.381, so the failure is not an artefact of a weaker classifier; the same input under random partitioning reaches 0.993 by placing test frames inside training inputs. A follow-up compression study finds that fourfold Doppler-bin aggregation raises recall on the held-out acquisition from 0.222 to 0.398 with no detected change in standard-split recall, alongside three negative or inconclusive results reported in full. We present this as a single-acquisition case study and argue that acquisition-level breakdowns should accompany aggregate accuracy on benchmarks assembled from continuous recordings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Automatic target recognition from frequency modulated continuous wave (FMCW) radar has been widely addressed with convolutional networks operating on range--Doppler representations. On the Real Doppler RAD-DAR database, the benchmark studied here, published classifiers report accuracies between 98 and 99\,\%~\cite{roldan2020,zhang2025}, and the reported figure is in both cases obtained by partitioning the available frames at random.
That partitioning choice deserves scrutiny. Radar datasets are assembled from continuous recordings, so consecutive frames within one acquisition are strongly correlated. A random partition places frames from a single acquisition on both sides of the split, and the reported accuracy therefore describes performance on acquisitions the model has already seen. It does not describe performance on a new deployment, a new day, or a new target trajectory, which is the quantity a system integrator actually needs.
This paper re-evaluates a widely used public dataset under a protocol that holds out whole acquisitions. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A reproducible preparation procedure for the Real Doppler RAD-DAR database that removes an exact duplication present in the distributed archive and recovers the acquisition structure from the directory hierarchy (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
\item A five-fold comparison of random against session-grouped partitioning, in which we do not establish a significant aggregate difference, together with a discussion of why such comparisons are difficult to test properly (Section~\ref{sec:leak}).
\item Identification of a single drone acquisition on which the classifier fails almost completely under acquisition-level evaluation, and a controlled exposure experiment associating that failure with the absence of comparable training examples (Section~\ref{sec:regime}).
\item Evidence that the failure is not an artefact of a weaker classifier. Rebuilding the published baseline's three-frame input reaches 0.979 aggregate accuracy and still fails on that acquisition; the same input under random partitioning reaches 0.993 by placing test frames inside training inputs (Section~\ref{sec:inputgap}).
\item A follow-up compression study in which fourfold Doppler aggregation improves transfer to the held-out acquisition with no detected change in standard-split accuracy, an effect established for that acquisition alone and not explained, alongside a mechanism test and an interaction test that are negative and inconclusive respectively (Section~\ref{sec:compress}).
\end{enumerate}
We emphasise the negative results deliberately: several physically motivated hypotheses were formulated during this work and measurement supported none of them, and reporting only the surviving result would misrepresent what the data supports. We also state the scope plainly. Both principal observations concern the same acquisition, so the effective sample size at the level of acquisitions is one. This is a case study of one benchmark, not a general result about radar target recognition.

\section{Related Work}
The Real Doppler RAD-DAR database was released with a convolutional baseline, DopplerNet, reporting 99.48\,\% accuracy on the three-class problem of vehicles, drones and pedestrians~\cite{roldan2020}. The acquisition hardware is described separately~\cite{quevedo2019}. A recent lightweight architecture reports 98.08\,\% average accuracy on the same data with a model footprint of roughly 400\,kB~\cite{zhang2025}, and provides the per-class confusion matrices we use as an external reference in Section~\ref{sec:baseline}. 
That correlated samples inflate apparent performance is not a new observation. Grouped partitioning is standard practice in human activity recognition, where subject-disjoint splits are routine, and radar recognition studies have used track-level separation to limit correlation between training and test segments~\cite{buchman2022}. Nor is the difficulty of conditions absent from training a new concern in radar: recent work addresses single-source domain generalisation for low-slow-small target recognition under changing conditions~\cite{chen2025}, and open-set recognition that rejects classes not present in training rather than forcing every echo into a trained class~\cite{bai2026}.
 We therefore do not claim that unseen regimes are an unstudied problem. Our contribution is an acquisition-level audit of one widely reused benchmark, localising a concrete failure regime that the benchmark's aggregate accuracy does not reveal, with a controlled experiment associating that failure with coverage rather than intrinsic difficulty.

Comparing learning procedures across resampled partitions is also known to be statistically delicate. Dietterich showed that several commonly used tests have inflated Type~I error on cross-validation scores~\cite{dietterich1998}, and Nadeau and Bengio showed that the dependence introduced by overlapping training sets must be accounted for in any variance estimate~\cite{nadeau2003}. We take both cautions seriously in Section~\ref{sec:leak} and deliberately do not rest any claim on a resampling-based significance test.

Separately, the observation that model compression damages some classes far more than others is established in the vision domain~\cite{hooker2019,hooker2020,tran2022}. Our compression study was originally motivated as a radar instantiation of that phenomenon; we report in Section~\ref{sec:compress} that we did not reproduce it.

\section{Dataset and Preparation}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Acquisition}

The database was collected with an FMCW system at 8.75\,GHz with 500\,MHz of bandwidth, using one transmitting and eight receiving antennas forming a 64-element array~\cite{roldan2020,quevedo2019}. The published parameters give a range resolution of 0.878\,m, a maximum unambiguous range of 3.598\,km and a Doppler resolution of 0.34\,km/h with 512 integrated ramps. We quote these rather than recomputing them: a naive calculation from bandwidth alone gives a range resolution roughly three times finer than the system achieves.

Samples are supplied as $11 \times 61$ matrices obtained by applying a constant false alarm rate detector to the full range--Doppler matrix and cropping around each detection. The retained Doppler window spans approximately 21\,km/h, considerably narrower than the extent associated with rotor blade returns from a small rotorcraft, so the crops do not retain what is needed to analyse blade-flash structure. This constrains what mechanism arguments the data can support, and is not a claim that the crops hold no micro-Doppler information at all.

\subsection{Duplication in the distributed archive}

The distributed archive contains the complete directory tree twice, once beneath a \texttt{data/} prefix and once at the archive root. Content hashing of each acquisition folder identifies and removes the duplication, after which the totals are 5720 vehicle, 5065 drone and 6700 pedestrian samples, or 17\,485 in total, matching the published figure exactly~\cite{zhang2025}. A second pass removes any folder whose name duplicates another of the same class but which holds fewer files, which guards against a truncated copy left by an interrupted extraction.

\subsection{Recovering acquisition structure}

Acquisition structure is encoded in the directory hierarchy rather than in file names, which are running indices: folders are named by acquisition time, with suffixes distinguishing variants recorded in the same session, for example \texttt{15-55}, \texttt{15-55a}, \texttt{15-55m} and \texttt{15-55p}. We group by the leading \texttt{HH-MM} timestamp, which is a fact about when the data was collected and cannot drift with a parameter choice, and which yields 15 vehicle, 21 drone and 16 pedestrian units, 52 in total, from 80 folders. An earlier attempt to group by content similarity failed badly and is worth recording as a caution: because every sample is cropped around its own detection, the mean feature vector of any acquisition resembles a central blob and all acquisitions correlate above 0.9 regardless of provenance.

The rule is an inference from directory structure, not a ground-truth acquisition identifier, and we treat it as an assumption. Two observations support it. The suffix pattern is systematic, and several suffixed pairs share a file count \emph{and} peak-to-floor statistics identical to one decimal place, for instance \texttt{17-09} and \texttt{17-09p} at 140 files and 41.4\,dB; every such pair falls within one unit under our rule. The rule is also conservative, since merging independent recordings can only make evaluation harder, and a reader preferring folder-level grouping can reproduce either choice from the released partition files.

\section{Model and Protocol}
\label{sec:baseline}

\subsection{Architecture and normalisation}

We use a compact depthwise-separable convolutional network of 101\,139 parameters, sized to match the 101\,419-parameter reference model reported on this dataset~\cite{zhang2025} so that its behaviour is representative of a network that would plausibly be deployed.

Input normalisation was selected by ablation over grouped folds at seed 0. A fixed affine offset reached 0.925 accuracy against 0.908 for peak-referenced normalisation, with global standardisation and a two-channel peak-plus-level scheme both at 0.922; the full ablation is in the released artefact. The offset scheme was additionally preferred because, being a fixed affine map, quantising before or after normalisation is equivalent up to a scale factor, which keeps the compression analysis of Section~\ref{sec:compress} interpretable.

\subsection{Partitioning}

Under the \emph{grouped} protocol, whole timestamp units are assigned to train, validation, or test; no unit contributes samples to more than one subset. Folds are balanced by sample count rather than unit count, since unit sizes range from 31 to 660 files. Under the \emph{random} protocol, individual frames are shuffled, with fold sizes matched per class to the
grouped folds.

Validation data is drawn from whole training units, never from training frames: validating on random frames under a grouped protocol would reintroduce the correlation the protocol exists to remove, and every early-stopping decision would then be taken on contaminated data. Splits are verified by content hash rather than by path, since duplicate folders make path-level verification unsound.

\section{Is Random Partitioning Optimistic?}
\label{sec:leak}

Table~\ref{tab:kfold} reports five folds by three seeds under each protocol. The design separates two sources of variation: \emph{fold} standard deviation measures sensitivity to which units are held out, and \emph{seed} standard deviation measures sensitivity to initialisation alone.

Under grouped partitioning, the mean accuracy is 0.9099 against 0.9335, and mean drone recall is 0.8376 against 0.9358. These differences are not trivial, but they are small relative to the fold-to-fold spread: the grouped standard deviation is 0.0427 for accuracy and 0.1389 for drone recall, roughly eight and eighteen times the random-partition figures.

We rest no claim on a significance test here. Fold scores under the two protocols are not naturally paired, since a grouped fold is defined by which acquisitions it contains while a random fold is defined by a shuffle, and cross-validation scores are dependent through overlapping training sets~\cite{dietterich1998,nadeau2003}; a test treating the folds as paired independent observations would overstate its confidence. For the record it gives $t=-1.19$ for accuracy and $t=-1.12$ for drone F1, neither significant even were the test sound. \textbf{We therefore report that we did not establish a significant aggregate difference between the protocols, which is a weaker statement than the absence of one.} What the data does show clearly is heterogeneity. The per-fold differences in drone F1 are $+0.003$, $-0.023$, $+0.007$, $-0.226$ and $-0.010$: the aggregate difference between protocols is dominated by a single fold rather than appearing as a consistent shift. Section~\ref{sec:regime} identifies what that fold contains.

Every other drone acquisition places its target energy at Doppler centroid between 29.7 and 31.2, at the centre of the 61-bin axis, and holds it there: the within-acquisition standard deviation ranges from 0.64 to 2.37 bins. Acquisition \texttt{13-48} places it at 8.50, and with a standard deviation of 10.64 bins, four and a half times the largest spread any other acquisition shows. It is separated from the other twenty on both where its target energy sits and how much that position varies. A nearest-centroid check finds 100\,\% of its samples closest to the drone class centroid, so a labelling error is not indicated.

The source publication describes three drone exercises: radial trajectories flown on autopilot to 3.1\,km, a circular trajectory at 800\,m range, and a free flight with speed and altitude varied manually~\cite{roldan2020}; the hardware paper likewise reports a free flight among its tests~\cite{quevedo2019}. Manual free flight can produce greater variation in radial velocity than an autopilot-flown radial or circular trajectory, which makes the observed Doppler spread consistent with that exercise; the actual distribution would depend on trajectory, orientation and radar geometry, none of which the release records. Acquisition \texttt{13-48} is the only one whose Doppler scatter is an order of magnitude above the rest. We therefore consider it the most likely candidate, while noting that the publication gives no timestamps and that a per-recording processing difference cannot be excluded from the published data alone.

Recall rises from 0.178 to 0.948 over ten seeds, a recovery of 0.770 (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}). Under the held-out condition 79\,\% of the samples are assigned to the pedestrian class; with half the acquisition in training, 3\,\% are. The effect is far larger than its seed-to-seed spread: held-out recall ranges from 0.142 to 0.264 across the ten seeds and the half-in-training condition from 0.904 to 0.980, so the two conditions do not overlap on any seed. The worst seed with half the acquisition in training still exceeds the best seed without it by 0.640. We lead with that figure rather than with a significance test because it assumes nothing about the distribution of either condition: on no seed do the two come near each other, and the separation is an order of magnitude larger than the spread within either condition.

The experiment indicates that the failure is associated with the absence of comparable training examples rather than with an irreducible inability to classify samples of this kind. We stop short of a stronger causal statement. Because the added training samples come from the same acquisition, the experiment cannot separate an out-of-distribution flight regime from an acquisition-specific signature that the network learns, nor from a per-recording processing difference; it establishes that something present in this acquisition and absent from the other twenty is learnable and sufficient.

\subsection{A control acquisition}

A large recovery is only evidence of a coverage failure if a large recovery is unusual. Acquisitions well below the aggregate exist in the other two classes: the weakest pedestrian and vehicle acquisitions sit at 0.7776 and 0.8189. We therefore repeated the identical experiment, at the same ten seeds, on the weakest acquisition in either non-drone class, \texttt{11-23}.

Held out entirely it reaches 0.800, and with half of it in training 0.856, a recovery of 0.056 against the 0.770 of \texttt{13-48}. We repeated this once more on \texttt{15-37}, the weakest vehicle acquisition, which recovers 0.008: its held-out and half-in-training conditions overlap on nine of ten seeds and the per-seed difference has a standard deviation of 0.030, so its recovery is not distinguishable from zero. All three acquisitions sit well below their class aggregate, and exposure repairs one while leaving the other two where they were. Low recall and a large recovery are therefore separate properties, and the coverage result is not a restatement of \texttt{13-48} being difficult.

The two controls also differ from \texttt{13-48} descriptively. \texttt{11-23} is unremarkable in Doppler and an outlier in extent, with nearly three times the cells within 20\,dB of its peak ($z=+3.4$), and 56.8\,\% of its frames sit nearest the \emph{drone} centroid; we read it as confusable with another class rather than unrepresented, a failure adding training data should not repair. \texttt{15-37} is the cleaner control: no property we measured separates it from the other vehicle acquisitions, the largest standardised difference across the twelve being $0.25$ against $-15.67$ for the Doppler centroid of \texttt{13-48}, and 62.7\,\% of its frames sit nearest their own class. It is descriptively ordinary and simply handled less well. An acquisition can therefore be weak without being unrepresented, which is the alternative the coverage experiment has to exclude, though three acquisitions of fifty-two remain three cases and we do not claim these modes exhaust the possibilities.

This is the paper's principal result. A classifier benchmarked on this dataset with random partitioning reports accuracy near 99\,\% while failing almost completely on one acquisition, and the standard evaluation gives no indication of it. If that acquisition corresponds to a distinct flight regime, as the Doppler evidence and the source publication's description together suggest, the practical consequence for a counter-UAS deployment is direct. We note that this consequence follows from an identification we cannot verify, and treat it accordingly.

\section{A Follow-Up Compression Study}
\label{sec:compress}

The experiments in this section were motivated by the gap identified in Section~\ref{sec:regime} and should be read as a targeted follow-up to it rather than as an independent study.

We examined two compression axes. \emph{Input resolution} aggregates adjacent Doppler bins by factors of 1, 2, 4 and 8, summing linear power and returning to decibels. This is incoherent aggregation and is \emph{not} equivalent to shortening the coherent processing interval, since the dataset supplies magnitude only and the required phase is absent; we report it strictly as a reduction of input tensor size, which is a real memory cost on an embedded target. \emph{Numerical precision} applies uniform quantisation to input and weights at 32, 16, 8 and 4 bits. Each cell is evaluated both on the standard grouped split and with \texttt{13-48} excluded from training and used as the sole test set.

The full $4\times4$ grid was run at two seeds and is in the released artefact; Table~\ref{tab:agg} reports a ten-seed follow-up restricted to 32 and 8 bits. Four-bit weight quantisation was unstable, with an accuracy standard deviation of 0.1757 across seeds, so we do not interpret its cells; 16-bit results lay within one standard deviation of 32-bit throughout.

Every condition in this grid is trained at the same ten seeds, and the seed fixes the partition and the initialisation together, so two conditions differ only in the input representation. The per-seed difference is therefore the unit of analysis and we test it directly, rather than treating the two sets of ten runs as independent samples, which would discard the pairing and overstate the residual variance.

Two features of Table~\ref{tab:agg} deserve comment. First, standard-split drone recall lies between 0.901 and 0.932 across every aggregation level and both bit widths. Comparing fourfold against full resolution on the standard split gives a paired $t$ of $1.05$ at 32 bits and $0.17$ at 8 bits over ten seeds ($p=0.32$ and $p=0.87$), so we do not detect a change; we note that this is a failure to detect a difference and not a demonstration of equivalence, which would require a non-inferiority margin we have not specified. Second, recall on the unseen acquisition is not flat at all: it rises from 0.222 at full resolution to 0.398 at fourfold aggregation and then collapses to 0.192 at eightfold, at 32 bits, with the same pattern at 8 bits.

A paired test on fourfold against full resolution gives $t=+4.24$ at 32 bits and $t=+4.08$ at 8 bits on nine degrees of freedom, with $p=0.002$ and $p=0.003$. The assumption-free form of the same statement is the more useful one: the per-seed difference is positive on ten of ten seeds at 32 bits and nine of ten at 8 bits, with a mean gain of 0.175 in unseen-regime recall. A bootstrap confidence interval on the difference of means, computed on the first five seeds, gave $[+0.049,+0.307]$ and $[+0.058,+0.392]$ respectively, both excluding zero. Per-seed values are consistent with a single wide distribution rather than a split one, so the effect is a shift in the mean accompanied by strong seed dependence, not a subset of runs succeeding while others fail.

\textbf{We therefore report that fourfold Doppler aggregation improves transfer to this held-out acquisition, with no detected change in standard-split recall and a fourfold reduction in input tensor size.} The qualifier is important: Section~\ref{sec:compress}C shows that the effect did not reappear on four other held-out acquisitions. The improvement is substantial in relative terms, close to a doubling, but leaves recall far below the 0.948 obtainable when the acquisition is represented in training (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}). It is a partial mitigation, not a solution.

\subsection{No consistent effect across the acquisitions tested}

To test whether this is a property of aggregation or of \texttt{13-48}, we repeated the comparison with five acquisitions held out one at a time, chosen to span easy and difficult cases rather than only the pathological one: \texttt{13-48} (drone, $N=393$), \texttt{15-37} (vehicle, 370), \texttt{15-21} (drone, 181), \texttt{12-34} (drone, 660) and \texttt{11-23} (pedestrian, 308). At three seeds each, the change in held-out recall from full resolution to fourfold aggregation was $+0.136$, $+0.048$, $+0.020$, $-0.017$ and $-0.022$ respectively, a mean of $+0.033$; the per-acquisition figures are in the released artefact.

Aggregation therefore improved three of the five, with a paired $t$ of $+1.16$ across acquisitions. An exact sign test gives $p=1.00$ two-sided, and $p=0.50$ against the directional alternative that aggregation helps. \textbf{No consistent effect was observed across the five acquisitions tested.} With five units the test has almost no power, so this is inconclusive about a general effect rather than evidence that none exists. What it does establish is narrower and sufficient for our purposes: the large improvement seen on \texttt{13-48} did not reappear on any of the other four, so the aggregation claim cannot be stated beyond that acquisition.

One structural feature should be noted: the four others begin between 0.800 and 0.934, leaving little room to improve, whereas \texttt{13-48} begins at 0.217 and reaches 0.353. The compatible reading is that aggregation recovered part of the gap in the one case where the classifier was failing. With a single failing acquisition we cannot distinguish an effect specific to failing acquisitions from one specific to this acquisition, and we do not claim the former.

\subsection{Two further negative results}

\emph{The experiment is inconclusive about interaction between the quantisation axes.} At four bits and fourfold aggregation, quantising the input alone costs 0.0159 in accuracy and the weights alone 0.1028, against a measured joint cost of 0.1412 and an additive prediction of 0.1187. The four conditions are fully crossed on seed, so the interaction contrast $I = Y_{\mathrm{both}} - Y_{\mathrm{input}} - Y_{\mathrm{weights}} + Y_{\mathrm{neither}}$ is formed within each seed and its spread taken across them: $I = -0.0225$, with a 95\,\% interval of $[-0.175, +0.141]$ and $t=-0.24$ on four degrees of freedom. We therefore found no evidence of an interaction, which is not additivity, and report the experiment as inconclusive rather than negative, the interval being wide because four-bit weight quantisation is unstable enough for individual seeds to collapse to 0.534. The per-cell figures are in the released artefact.

\emph{Doppler-shift augmentation does not reproduce the effect.} Since \texttt{13-48} is distinguished chiefly by the Doppler position of its target energy (Table~\ref{tab:profile}), aggregation might help by making the classifier less sensitive to that position; if so, augmenting training with Doppler shifts should reproduce the effect at full resolution. Shifts fill vacated cells with the sample's own noise floor rather than wrapping, since a circular shift is not physical. At shifts of $\pm4$, $\pm8$ and $\pm16$ bins, unseen-regime recall was 0.1608, 0.1613 and 0.1603 against a baseline of 0.2214: consistently slightly worse, while standard-split recall was unaffected. This explanation is therefore not supported, and we put it no more strongly than that: the experiment rules out the one implementation of Doppler invariance we tried, not every route by which positional sensitivity might matter. We report the aggregation effect as an empirical finding and offer no mechanism for it.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\subsection{What the results support}

The coverage result is the finding we consider established: a measured effect of 0.77 in recall, a controlled manipulation isolating the cause, and an independent descriptive signature identifying the acquisition without reference to classifier performance. The recommendation follows, with its scope stated. For datasets assembled from continuous acquisitions, where deployment requires generalisation to recordings absent from training, aggregate accuracy should be accompanied by an acquisition-level breakdown, which is a by-product of a single cross-validation pass. Where deployment genuinely operates on the benchmark's own distribution, frame-level evaluation may be appropriate and we make no claim against it.

The aggregation result is established for one acquisition, is not understood, and does not reproduce on four others. We report it because a reader repeating this audit will want to know the comparison was run and how it came out, and because the asymmetry it shows is worth testing where more than one failing acquisition is available. We do not present it as a recommendation for practice.

\subsection{What they do not support}

Four physically motivated mechanisms were formulated to explain why the drone class should be more fragile, and measurement supported none: rotor sideband loss, which the Doppler span of the supplied crops does not let us assess; low redundancy from compact energy, which did not hold once concentration was measured relative to the peak rather than to total power; limited dynamic-range headroom, where the apparent correlation with per-acquisition recall (Pearson $-0.62$) falls to $-0.16$ once \texttt{13-48} is excluded; and Doppler positional insensitivity, which the augmentation experiment did not reproduce. In each case the measurement failed to support the explanation rather than excluding the underlying physics, which our data in several cases cannot reach. The pattern is itself informative: plausible physical stories about this dataset are easy to construct and difficult to sustain.

\subsection{Limitations}

Both findings rest on the same single acquisition. The coverage result concerns \texttt{13-48} directly, and the aggregation result is measured by transfer to \texttt{13-48}, so the paper has an effective sample size of one at the level of flight regimes. Whether fourfold aggregation would improve transfer to a \emph{different} unrepresented regime is untested, and we cannot claim it. The data comes from one dataset, one radar and one drone platform, and the acquisition comprises 7.8\,\% of the drone samples. Whether the distinguishing Doppler offset reflects flight dynamics or a per-recording processing difference cannot be determined from the published data. The identification of \texttt{13-48} with the free-flight exercise is an inference, since the source publication does not supply acquisition timestamps. The two controls in Section~\ref{sec:regime}D show that the large recovery was not observed in either of them, but three acquisitions of fifty-two have been tested this way, and the contrast between them is a contrast of three cases rather than a general law about difficult recordings. Our grouping rule assumes that a shared timestamp prefix implies a shared acquisition. Section~\ref{sec:data}C sets out the evidence for it, but it remains an inference from directory structure rather than a ground-truth acquisition identifier.

Section~\ref{sec:compress}C found no consistent effect of aggregation across the five acquisitions tested, and we state that as a non-reproduction on four specific acquisitions rather than as the absence of a general effect, since five units cannot support the stronger reading in either direction. The aggregation claim is accordingly specific to \texttt{13-48} throughout. Whether the effect would appear for a different acquisition on which the classifier fails cannot be tested within this dataset, which contains only one such acquisition.

\subsection{The published baselines use an input the release does not preserve}
\label{sec:inputgap}

Our classifier reaches 0.9335 under random partitioning, below the 99.48\,\% reported for DopplerNet and the 98.08\,\% for the lighter reference. We measured that gap rather than conceding it. Roughly two points are protocol, not model: 0.9335 is a five-fold cross-validated mean, while both published figures are single-partition quantities, and the same architecture on a single random partition reaches 0.9537. Capacity and schedule explain none of the rest. Across width multipliers of one, two and three, to 871\,731 parameters, and 40 and 80 epochs, the best configuration reaches 0.9546, which is 0.0009 above the architecture we use; accuracy plateaus, so the model is not capacity limited here.

The remaining 2.6 points are not capacity either. They are the input. DopplerNet does not classify a single frame: its input is an $11 \times 61 \times 3$ distance-Doppler-time tensor, three time-consecutive detections stacked as channels and separated by 400\,ms, a choice the source publication justifies through the classification latency each added frame costs~\cite{roldan2020}. Our model, and every experiment above, sees one frame. That is a difference in available information rather than in model size.

It is also not a difference a user of the published release can remove. The distributed archive supplies one CFAR-cropped matrix per detection, with no timestamp and no frame counter; the only ordering it preserves is the trailing integer in each file name, and that integer is not a reliable time axis. The 400\,ms triplets the published model consumed are therefore not reconstructible from the public data, by us or by anyone else working from the same release. We treat this as part of the paper's subject rather than an aside. A headline accuracy obtained from an input that the public release does not retain cannot be reproduced from that release by anyone, which is a reporting problem of the same family as the one this paper is about.

We can nevertheless build the closest approximation the release supports and, crucially, measure whether that approximation carries any temporal information rather than assuming it. Triplets are formed within each folder from consecutive file-name integers, never across a gap in the numbering, which yields 17\,221 triplets from 17\,485 frames and 391 on \texttt{13-48}. Three arms are compared at ten shared seeds, on the same triplet centres so that the comparison is paired sample for sample (Table~\ref{tab:temporal}): \emph{single}, the centre frame alone; \emph{index}, the centre with its two index-adjacent neighbours; and \emph{shuffle}, the centre flanked by two frames drawn at random from the same folder and never its true neighbours. The shuffle arm holds channel count, folder, labels and sample count fixed and removes only adjacency, which makes the ordering assumption testable instead of load-bearing. Replacing the stem convolution to accept three channels takes the model from 101\,139 to 102\,291 parameters.

Two results follow, and the first answers the objection this subsection exists to address. A three-frame input raises random-split accuracy from 0.9512 to 0.9785, within a quarter of a point of the 0.9808 reported for the lighter published model under a comparable single partition. That classifier still fails on \texttt{13-48}: recall under the held-out protocol rises from 0.209 to 0.381, a paired gain of $+0.171$ over ten seeds ($t=+6.93$), and so remains a condition in which the majority of the acquisition's frames are assigned to the wrong class. \textbf{The failure is therefore not an artefact of a single-frame input or of a classifier weaker than those published.} Even the shuffle arm, at 0.9934, leaves \texttt{13-48} at 0.609, far below both the 0.90 to 0.99 of other acquisitions and the 0.948 obtainable once the acquisition is represented in training. Every configuration we tested fails on it.

The second result we did not anticipate, and it concerns the benchmark rather than our model. The shuffle arm does not merely match the index arm, it beats it, by 0.228 on \texttt{13-48} over ten paired seeds ($t=-3.93$). Since the arms differ only in whether companions are index-adjacent, a null would have meant file-name order carried nothing; it is not null, so the ordering does carry structure, and index-adjacent frames are measurably more alike than frames drawn from across the same acquisition. More importantly, accuracy rises monotonically with how widely each arm draws its companions, 0.9512 to 0.9785 to 0.9934, while the seed standard deviation collapses from 0.0030 to 0.0007. That is the signature of overlap rather than of learning: a sliding window plus a frame-level random partition places test frames inside training inputs, and since neighbouring detections are nearly identical, a test frame whose neighbour was seen in training is close to a memorised one. The single-frame arm cannot leak this way, its input being one frame carrying its own split tag. We therefore do not offer 0.9934 as a result but as a demonstration of how readily a multi-frame input and a random partition manufacture near-perfect accuracy here, reaching the published figure to within 0.0014 by a route involving no better generalisation at all.

\section{Conclusion}

Re-evaluating a public radar target recognition benchmark at the level of acquisitions leaves aggregate accuracy close to that obtained under random partitioning, and we do not establish a significant aggregate difference. The per-acquisition breakdown is nevertheless strongly heterogeneous: one drone acquisition of twenty-one attains 0.249 out-of-fold recall against 0.90 to 0.99 for the remainder, and excluding it from training entirely leaves recall on a held-back half at 0.178, rising to 0.948 once the other half is added, with the two conditions overlapping on no seed. The same experiment recovers 0.056 and 0.008 on the weakest acquisition of each other class. The acquisition is distinguished by the Doppler position of its target energy, which we suggest, without being able to verify it, corresponds to the manually flown free-flight exercise described in the source publication. The failure survives a three-frame input matching the published baseline's formulation, so it is not a property of a weaker classifier.

A follow-up compression study found that fourfold Doppler-bin aggregation raises recall on this held-out acquisition from 0.222 to 0.398 with no detected change in standard-split recall. Three further results are negative or inconclusive and are reported in full: the mechanism we tested was not supported, the effect did not reappear on four other acquisitions, and we found no evidence of interaction between the quantisation axes in a test too weak to have detected a moderate one.

The practical conclusion is narrow and, we believe, sound: on benchmarks assembled from continuous acquisitions, a headline accuracy is compatible with near-complete failure on an individual acquisition, and a per-acquisition breakdown is inexpensive insurance against reporting one without knowing it. We present this as a case study of one dataset and one acquisition, and it should be read at that scope.

\section*{Reproducibility}

All preparation, partitioning, training and analysis code, the generated partition files and the per-stage reports are released at \url{https://github.com/falcon-colab/rdrd-acquisition-coverage}, archived at~\cite{dataset2026}. Every number reported here is recorded there in machine-readable form, each paired with the command that produces it and a tolerance set from the spread we report, alongside a script that reruns the experiments and checks each number against it.
